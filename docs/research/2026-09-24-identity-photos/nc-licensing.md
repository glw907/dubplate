# CC NonCommercial images in dubplate's embedded binary — research report

*This is research to inform a decision, not legal advice. Dubplate should get its own counsel before shipping any third-party creative work, especially at scale to strangers.*

## Recommendation

Do not embed CC-NC (BY-NC, BY-NC-SA) images in the dubplate binary. NC assets make the combined work non-free by the standards dubplate's own downstream (Debian, Fedora, and any future OSI-badge or distro packaging) will apply, and dubplate's stated goal is "no site-specific value... outside `internal/config`" plus a strangers-download-and-run product — that audience includes people running paid hosting, distro packagers, and container-registry maintainers who cannot legally accept an NC-encumbered binary into free channels even though *dubplate itself* almost certainly is not "commercial" under CC's own test. The clean path is Option (a) from Q5: ship only CC0/PD/BY/BY-SA photographs in the binary itself (with TASL captions satisfying attribution law), and, if the NC photographs matter enough to keep, put them in a separate, clearly-labeled optional asset pack that an operator fetches or downloads themselves — never bundled into the compiled artifact, never required. This keeps the Go binary itself unambiguously free/open-source, avoids becoming the test case for "is a free hobby project commercial," and avoids ever entangling BY-SA's share-alike clause with binary code. The grayscale/grain/vignette treatment is legally irrelevant to this decision: NC permits derivatives regardless, so the only place the treatment matters is if ND images were ever considered, and even there a CSS filter applied at render time (not baked into the shipped file) is the safer reading, though CC's own guidance leaves this genuinely uncertain.

## Q1. What does CC NC actually prohibit? Is a FOSS project "commercial"? Does third-party paid redistribution change that? Where does liability sit?

**CC's own definition.** Every CC NC variant (BY-NC 2.0–4.0, BY-NC-SA) defines NonCommercial identically. From the BY-NC 4.0 legal code, Section 1(9): "NonCommercial means not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation." (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/legalcode.en)

**CC's guidance on what that means in practice**, from CC's own wiki interpretation page: "NonCommercial turns on the use, not the identity of the reuser" — meaning a nonprofit can make a commercial use of an NC work, and a for-profit company can make a noncommercial use of one. CC explicitly declines to give a bright-line rule: "There will always be uses that are challenging to categorize as commercial or noncommercial. CC cannot advise you on what is and is not commercial use." (https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/NonCommercial_interpretation; also stated on the general FAQ, https://creativecommons.org/faq/)

**Is dubplate itself "commercial"?** dubplate is free, OSI-licensed code, given away with no monetary compensation sought and no primary commercial-advantage purpose — on the plain "primarily intended for or directed towards" test, the project's own act of embedding and distributing the image is very likely noncommercial. The word "primarily" was deliberately added in the 4.0 license family precisely to avoid disqualifying incidental commercial contact (CC's "Defining Noncommercial" study, https://creativecommons.org/2009/09/14/defining-noncommercial-report-published/; report PDF at https://mirrors.creativecommons.org/defining-noncommercial/Defining_Noncommercial_fullreport.pdf).

**Does a third party's paid use flip it?** This is the real exposure, and CC's license text is explicit that NC riders travel with the material, not the original distributor's intent. Because dubplate ships as source that strangers compile and run, and because the images travel in the compiled binary, every downstream act of distribution or use is its own separate exercise of the license — a company embedding dubplate in a paid hosting product, a paid "dubplate-as-a-service" operator, or (per this repo's own CLAUDE.md) a Linux distribution vendor packaging the binary, each independently has to satisfy "not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage" for *their own* act of use/redistribution. A paid host or a commercial distro repackaging a binary containing NC photographs for profit is squarely the case NC is written to prohibit — CC's guidance ("uses that nonprofit entities can make... and not be viewed as violating the NC term") cuts the other way for anyone charging money.

**Liability.** CC's own FAQ and legal code place enforcement on whoever is exercising the license at the time — i.e., each redistributor is separately liable for their own noncommercial-compliance failure; there is no "upstream absolves downstream" provision. Practically, this means: (1) dubplate's own act of shipping the binary carries real but probably low risk, since the project itself is noncommercial; (2) the risk shifts to and multiplies at every paid or commercial redistribution point dubplate cannot control, and dubplate's maintainers would be the ones fielding a takedown/infringement claim from the photographer if that downstream misuse is traced back to assets dubplate baked into its own artifact, because dubplate is the party that made the initial unauthorized (from a strict-NC reading) redistribution possible at scale. CC states plainly it offers no legal advice on this ("CC cannot advise you") and that ambiguity itself is the liability: an aggressive rights holder can plausibly argue that a project designed to be run by paid hosts and packaged by commercial distro vendors is not "primarily noncommercial" in effect, even if the maintainers' own motive is noncommercial.

## Q2. Does bundling an NC asset make the combined distribution non-free/non-OSI? Can assets carry a separate license? Real examples.

**OSI/FSF position.** The Open Source Definition's criterion 6, "No Discrimination Against Fields of Endeavor," reads: "The license must not restrict anyone from making use of the program in a specific field of endeavor." (https://opensource.org/osd) Criterion 1, "Free Redistribution," bars restricting resale as part of an aggregate distribution. An NC restriction is a direct violation of criterion 6 by design — commercial use is exactly the "field of endeavor" it forbids — which is why "any license that restricts commercial use is fundamentally incompatible with open source principles" per OSI's stated posture, and why CC itself does not submit its NC variants to OSI for approval. The FSF's parallel free-software definition (freedom 0, "run the program... for any purpose") is violated the same way; the FSF and Debian both treat NC works as non-free content, not non-free software per se, but the practical effect on a *combined* distribution is the same: whether the whole falls out of "free" depends on how tightly the NC asset is bound to the free code (see Q5 on BY-SA/aggregation below for the more nuanced version of this same question).

**Does bundling contaminate the whole?** Not automatically — the OSD and DFSG evaluate a work's *license*, and a repository/binary can, and commonly does, contain material under multiple licenses side by side, as long as each file's terms are clear and the code's own license is not itself infected. What bundling an NC asset *does* do is make the combined artifact as distributed no longer wholly under an OSI-approved or DFSG-free license — the asset itself remains non-free, and any distributor (including dubplate's own release binary) is bound by both licenses simultaneously. This is why the practice everywhere is a **separate assets license**, called out explicitly in project docs, so downstream packagers can make an informed choice per-file rather than treating the whole tree as a single license.

**Real examples of this separation:**
- Game engines/games routinely split code and art licenses. As one guide on OSS licensing for game art puts it: "Artwork and code can be separately licensed — art can be CC-BY-SA, but code can be another license... just include a clear statement of what license applies to what materials," recommending the split be file/folder-clean so a downstream consumer can say "this specific set of folders are CC licensed, and the rest are using this other code license." (https://blog.lazerwalker.com/2022/06/14/oss-licensing-for-art.html)
- A concrete named example: the Godot-engine game *Demon* ships source under MIT while distributing some assets under CC-BY-NC 4.0 — the same shape as dubplate's proposal, and the search summary notes explicitly that "CC-BY-NC forbids commercial use entirely and is unsuitable for any commercial game," i.e. the split is precisely what lets the commercial-use question apply only to the art files, not the whole codebase.
- Debian's own archive structure formalizes this at distribution scale: packages split into `main` (DFSG-free), `contrib` (free but depends on non-free), and `non-free`/`non-free-firmware` (non-free content, distributed anyway as a courtesy) — "Software packaged for Debian is normally classified into one of four categories" (https://wiki.debian.org/DebianFreeSoftwareGuidelines; https://www.debian.org/legal/licenses/). Data/content-only packages containing non-free fonts, game data, or documentation routinely live in `non-free` while the DFSG-free software that uses them lives in `main` and merely recommends/depends on the non-free companion — this is the standard mechanism, not an edge case.

## Q3. Do Debian, Fedora, Homebrew, and container registries accept NC assets inside a package?

- **Debian.** DFSG-incompatible content (including all CC-BY-NC-SA variants, per the Debian wiki's license table) cannot go in `main`; it can only ship in the `non-free` section, which most Debian derivatives (and Debian's own default install) omit, and which is a deliberately smaller, second-class distribution channel: "Debian-legal reviewed the CC licenses and concluded that none of the Creative Commons core [NC-bearing] licenses were free according to the DFSG and recommended that works released under these licenses 'should not be included in Debian'" outside `non-free` (https://wiki.debian.org/DFSGLicenses; https://www.debian.org/legal/licenses/). By contrast, current CC BY 3.0/4.0 and CC BY-SA 3.0/4.0 are explicitly listed on that same page as DFSG-free — so BY and BY-SA photographs would package cleanly into Debian `main`; NC will not.
- **Fedora.** Fedora's licensing process requires every distributed bit to be under an FSF- or OSI-style free license (Fedora's own summary states packages "not licensed under one of the licenses explicitly approved by the FSF or the OSI cannot be included in main or contrib" per project convention); Fedora has no analogue of Debian's `non-free` section for arbitrary NC content bundled inside a package — non-free content generally has to be excluded from the package entirely or handled as an external, not-shipped dependency. (Primary source page, `docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/legal/license-approval/`, was not directly fetchable in this session — it returned an anti-bot challenge page — so this is stated with lower confidence than the Debian finding and should be spot-checked against Fedora's `Licensing:Main`/`Licensing:SoftwareTypes` wiki pages before relying on it.)
- **Homebrew.** Homebrew's `License-Guidelines` doc (`docs.brew.sh/License-Guidelines`) was likewise not directly fetchable in this session (redirect only resolved). Homebrew formulae are typically build recipes rather than bundled binaries with embedded content, so the sharpest version of this question (an NC image baked into a shipped artifact) is less central to Homebrew's own review than to Debian/Fedora's, but Homebrew does reject/flag non-free-license software from core taps historically; this should be verified directly before relying on it.
- **Container registries (Docker Hub, GHCR, etc.).** These are not curation bodies for license purity the way Debian/Fedora are — Docker Hub's own terms state "Third-Party Products or content available in a registry or repository are subject to the corresponding third-party's license or terms" (docker.com legal), i.e. the registry itself imposes no DFSG/OSD-style gate; an NC-encumbered image would technically be *hostable* on a container registry with no automatic rejection. The exposure there shifts from "will it be accepted" to "who is liable when someone pulls and runs it commercially," which loops back to Q1.

**What this means for dubplate being packaged later.** If dubplate wants a Debian `main` package, a Fedora package, or generally to be treated as a normal free-software project by any distro, NC images baked into the binary either block that packaging outright or force the packager to strip/patch the assets out — a maintenance burden dubplate would be pushing onto every future packager, which cuts directly against the project's stated "ease of setup by a stranger" and no-special-defaults goals. Container-registry distribution alone (Docker image, no distro package) would technically still work, but at the cost of dubplate never becoming distro-packageable without a rebuild.

## Q4. Does grayscale/grain/vignette create an adaptation? Does it matter for NC vs ND? Can ND images be shown with a runtime CSS filter?

**Adaptation, generally.** CC BY-NC 4.0 §1(1) defines Adapted Material as material "in which the Licensed Material is translated, altered, arranged, transformed, or otherwise modified in a manner requiring permission under the Copyright and Similar Rights held by the Licensor" — the operative test is whether the modification would require the rights-holder's permission under ordinary copyright law absent the license, i.e. it is a derivative-work-law question, not a CC-specific one. A stylistic image edit — desaturation, grain overlay, vignette — is a textbook example of a derivative-work transformation (comparable to colorization/decolorization treatments courts have treated as derivative works), so it should be assumed to count as an adaptation for copyright purposes generally.

**Does it matter for NC?** No. Every CC license as of 4.0 — including all NC variants (BY-NC, BY-NC-SA) — explicitly permits adaptations; NC only restricts the *purpose* (commercial vs. non-commercial) of any use, adapted or not. So the grayscale/grain/vignette treatment is a non-issue for NC licensing specifically: applying it neither triggers nor avoids any NC problem.

**ND, by contrast, is the license where this matters.** ND (NoDerivatives) forbids sharing adapted material, full stop — but the current (4.0) CC legal code carves out "technical modifications": "simply making modifications authorized by [format/media adaptation] never produces Adapted Material," and the 4.0 text authorizes "making technical modifications necessary to exercise the Licensed Rights in other media and formats." Format-shifting (e.g., re-encoding, resizing, changing file format) is squarely inside that carve-out; a deliberate aesthetic transformation like grayscale-plus-grain-plus-vignette is a stylistic choice, not a technical necessity of displaying the image in a browser, and is very unlikely to fall inside that narrow exception — CC's own guides describe ND as barring sharing adapted material generally and don't extend the technical-modification carve-out to cosmetic filters (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nd/4.0/deed.en; https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/legalcode).

**Can an ND image be shown with a CSS filter applied at render time instead of baked into the file?** This is the strongest argument available for using ND images at all, but it is not a settled safe harbor — treat it as "probably better, not clearly safe." The reasoning: if the *shipped file* is the unmodified original and the visual transformation happens only in the viewer's browser via CSS at render time, arguably no "modified... material" is ever fixed, copied, or distributed — the licensee is redistributing the unaltered original, and the transformation is more like a display technique (akin to a browser's dark-mode inversion or a monitor's color profile) than a derivative work capture. Against that: the transformation is authored, deliberate, and part of the shipped product's presentation logic (the CSS itself, shipped in the binary, encodes the adaptation), so a rights holder could argue the "adapted" *experience* the software delivers is still the point being distributed, even though the bytes of the image file are untouched. CC's own guidance does not address this scenario directly; it is a genuinely open question rather than a documented rule, so this report cannot certify it as safe — the practical recommendation is not to use ND images for this treatment regardless, since NC images (which the owner already wants, and which raise no adaptation problem at all) sidestep the question entirely, and dubplate's separate `own-visual-identity` memory rule already means the treatment is a house design choice with no reference to match, so the ND question is avoidable rather than necessary.

## Q5. The alternatives

**(a) Ship only PD/CC0/BY/BY-SA in the binary; NC images as a separate optional download.** This is the cleanest option and matches the real-world pattern from Q2 (Godot's *Demon*, Debian's `main`/`non-free` split): the binary and its embedded assets stay wholly free/OSI-clean and distro-packageable, while an operator who wants the NC photographs opts in explicitly, at their own risk, understanding they are the one making the "use." Recommended.

**(b) Fetch NC images at runtime from their source URL.** This avoids ever *redistributing* the image (arguably making dubplate itself not a licensee of the image at all, just a client fetching from the rights holder's own host, similar to hotlinking), but it trades a licensing risk for several operational ones the owner should weigh explicitly:
- **Hotlinking**: dependent entirely on the source host (Flickr, Wikimedia, etc.) keeping the image at a stable URL indefinitely; link rot silently breaks the login page for every self-hosted instance.
- **Privacy/CSP**: every page load leaks each self-hoster's visitor IP and request metadata (referer, user-agent) to a third-party host outside the operator's control — a privacy regression for a private family/friend server whose entire point is not exposing member activity externally — and it forces every operator's Content-Security-Policy to allow an external image origin, widening the attack surface.
- **Availability**: the login page (a user-path page per this repo's `see-it-before-geoff-tests-it` bar) now has an external network dependency for something as basic as rendering; if the remote host is down, rate-limits, or geoblocks, the login page degrades for every operator simultaneously.
Given dubplate's stated goal of a simple, dependency-light, self-hosted product for strangers, (b) is workable only as a last resort and is not obviously better than (a).

**(c) Get explicit written permission from the rights holder.** This is legally the cleanest of all three: a bespoke grant from the photographer supersedes CC entirely and can be scoped exactly to dubplate's use (embedding, adapting with a tone filter, redistribution by any downstream party) — CC's own FAQ explicitly recommends this path when in doubt ("you should either contact the rights holder for clarification, or search for works that permit commercial uses"). The cost is per-image outreach and recordkeeping (a signed grant per photograph), which does not scale as well as picking from an already-free pool, but for a handful of curated period photographs central to the product's identity, it may be worth the one-time effort — and unlike NC, a custom grant can explicitly authorize third-party commercial redistribution, closing the exact gap Q1 identifies.

**BY-SA's share-alike interaction with a binary.** This is a distinct question from NC and matters if the owner considers BY-SA images (which *are* DFSG-free and OSI-non-code-compatible) rather than assumes BY-SA is NC-like. Share-alike requires that if you distribute Adapted Material, you must license *that adaptation* under the same (or a compatible) CC license — but it does not require that everything distributed alongside the image (the Go binary, the web app) becomes share-alike. CC and the Debian community draw this distinction as "adaptation" vs. "collection/aggregation": an image simply embedded unmodified into a larger software work, displayed by that software, is generally treated as a *collection* (the image and the code remain separate, independently-copyrightable works merely bundled together), not an adaptation of the code by the image or vice versa — this is the same logic behind Debian's `main`+`non-free` split and GPL's own "mere aggregation" carve-out, and it is why Demon's MIT code is not infected by its CC-BY-NC art. The debian-legal mailing list has specifically litigated a near-identical case — visual novels using CC BY-NC-SA background music — over exactly this question: whether the combined work is a share-alike-triggering adaptation or a free-to-mix aggregation (https://lists.debian.org/debian-legal/2014/11/msg00010.html); the community view leans toward aggregation being safe *if the image file itself is not modified and not merged into the code*, but the moment dubplate *adapts* the BY-SA image (the grayscale/grain/vignette treatment squarely counts, per Q4), that specific adapted image file must itself carry a BY-SA-compatible license when redistributed — it does not drag the Go binary into BY-SA, but it does mean the *modified JPEG/PNG* dubplate ships is itself now a BY-SA-licensed derivative that any downstream user is equally free to further adapt and redistribute under BY-SA. That is a real but containable obligation (attribution + share-alike on the image file only), unlike NC's blanket commercial-use prohibition.

## Q6. What attribution does CC BY require in practice for images in software?

**TASL** (Title, Author, Source, License) is CC's own recommended shorthand, not license text itself, for satisfying the license's attribution clause. CC's "Recommended practices for attribution" page and the BY-NC 4.0 legal code §3(a) (quoted in Q1 above) spell out what the license actually requires when you reshare the material: retain (i) identification of the creator(s), (ii) a copyright notice, (iii) a notice referring to the license itself, (iv) a notice referring to the disclaimer of warranties, (v) a URI/hyperlink to the material "to the extent reasonably practicable," and (vi) for adapted material, an indication that you modified it (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/legalcode.en §3(a); TASL practice guide at https://wiki.creativecommons.org/wiki/Recommended_practices_for_attribution).

**Where it must appear.** CC's guidance is explicitly medium-flexible: "provide the attribution in any reasonable manner based on the medium, means, and context" — there is no single mandated placement. CC's guides do specify that if you're providing the material via a medium that allows for it, you should include the attribution information "in, or together with," the material, and that the license text/URI must be reasonably discoverable alongside it.

**Does an on-image caption + a CREDITS file satisfy it?** Yes, in the reading this report is comfortable recommending: the on-image caption (which the owner already plans — "captioned with its photographer and license") plausibly covers creator identification and title if included; a companion CREDITS/NOTICE file bundled with the software satisfies the remainder (copyright notice, license reference, license text or URI, source link, and — if the image is adapted, which the tone treatment makes it — a statement that it was modified). Splitting a short visible caption on-screen from a fuller CREDITS file is standard practice and matches TASL's own flexibility guidance; the one item to double check per-image is the modification notice for BY-SA images specifically, since that is a license *requirement* (§3(a)(1)(C) in the BY-NC 4.0 text, structurally identical across the BY family) and easy to omit if the caption only says "photo by X, CC BY-SA" without noting "grayscale/grain/vignette treatment applied."
