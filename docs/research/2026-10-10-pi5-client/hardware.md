# Build a Pi 5 stereo client for about $850

Buy a 24-inch Dell P2424HT touch monitor on an Ergotron LX Pro arm, a roughly $100 USB DAC (Topping D10s, with SMSL SU-2 as the equal-priced alternate), a 1 TB NVMe drive in an Argon NEO 5 case, and run Raspberry Pi OS Trixie with PipeWire, Navidrome over an rclone-synced FLAC folder, the Feishin client with mpv, and raspotify for Spotify Connect. That totals about **$850 to $1,000 excluding the Pi**, depending on how much you spend on the arm. The money is concentrated on the one place it is visibly better, the screen and its mounting. Past about $100 a DAC buys nothing audible: Topping's D10s reads **107 to 110 dB SINAD** on Audio Science Review (ASR) and is recommended there ([ASR](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/topping-d10s-usb-dac-and-bridge-review.14859/)), and double-blind work found no audible gap between CD-quality and high-resolution paths ([AES](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=14195)). The evidence is thinner than the pick suggests. Nobody has published a Pi 5 + labwc test of any candidate touch monitor, Dell's own support page limits Linux to **3-point touch**, and the arm and several prices are unconfirmed. Treat the display and arm as a purchase with a return window, and run the verification list below the day the boxes arrive.

## The screen and arm are the only place extra money shows

The Dell P2424HT is a 23.8-inch, 1920x1080 IPS touch monitor at 300 cd/m2 with a 100x100 VESA pattern, **3.86 kg (8.5 lb) without its stand**, and HDMI, DisplayPort and USB-C inputs ([PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2063582/dell-p2424ht-review.html), [Monitornerds](https://monitornerds.com/dell-p2424ht-review/)). It is the largest panel that still hangs on a consumer arm. Going bigger adds weight, glare area and shelf footprint with no extra pixels at 1080p; no source quantifies where larger starts to hurt, so that cutoff is my judgment, and 27-inch touch monitors were not researched. Street pricing is murky: launch reviews put it at $419.99 to $519.99, and one US business reseller lists $468 against a $562.49 MSRP, undated ([SHI](https://portal.shi.com/product/46557041/Dell-P2424HT-LED-monitor)). Budget **$450**. Retailer specs list an anti-glare front polarizer, which matters on a shelf near a lamp, but one Slovak listing's "mat" is the only finish statement beyond that ([Sumillion](https://shop.sumillion.com/dell-p-series-p2424ht-computer-monitor-60-5-cm-23-8-1920-x-1080-pixels-full-hd-lcd-touchscreen-black-silver-p2424ht.html)).

The Pi 5's USB-C port cannot carry video, so the cabling is micro-HDMI to HDMI for the picture plus USB-A to USB-C for touch. That is one of the three connections Dell's own knowledge-base article lists as working ([Dell KB 000225948](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-cy/000225948/touch-function-does-not-work-on-dell-p2424ht?lang=en)). One MagicMirror forum user reached a working Pi setup only after a messier adapter chain with a powered HDMI-to-USB-C adapter, and that post is flagged deleted in the listing, so treat it as a warning rather than a recipe ([MagicMirror forum](https://forum.magicmirror.builders/post/127552)).

The Linux touch question is the biggest open risk, and the sources conflict. A search summary of Dell's usage guide says "10-point Touch for Windows and macOS, 3-point Touch for Linux" ([Dell KB 000217453](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-us/000217453/dell-p2424ht-monitor-usage-and-troubleshooting-guide)); I could not open the page itself, and a retailer spec sheet repeats the split. Against that, a blog test reports full 10-point multitouch on Ubuntu 24.04 in GNOME Wayland and KDE X11 ([Linuxano](https://linuxano.com/best-touchscreen-monitors-for-linux-tested/)). My reading is that 3-point is Dell's validation scope and the controller presents ordinary HID multitouch, but nothing confirms it on a Pi 5. For a music app, three points are enough (tap, scroll, drag); ten matter only for pinch gestures you will not use. The alternatives are the ViewSonic TD2455 (24-inch, 10-point, 100x100 VESA, about $400 to $545, vendor-claimed Linux support) and the iiyama ProLite T2454MSC, which also lists Linux support; neither has a Pi report ([Touchboards](https://www.touchboards.com/viewsonic/series_viewsonic-touch-displays), [PcComponentes](https://www.pccomponentes.com/monitor-iiyama-prolite-t2452msc-w1-238-ips-full-hd-tactil)). The $80 official Touch Display 2 is the only first-party option, but at 10 inches and portrait-native it fails the "as large as practical" test ([Linuxiac](https://linuxiac.com/raspberry-pi-launches-new-10-inch-touch-display-2-for-80/)). Waveshare's 15.6-inch HDMI panel is about $167 but weighs 1.5 to 1.9 kg, below the minimum load of most arms, and carries no brightness rating ([Waveshare wiki](https://www.waveshare.com/wiki/15.6inch_HDMI_LCD_(H)_(with_case))).

For the arm I recommend the **Ergotron LX Pro at about $199**. The older LX 45-241-026 (5 to 25 lb, 10 to 60 mm clamp thickness) still shows at $249 to $275 at some US sellers, but another dealer page labels it discontinued, so availability is mixed ([Staples](https://www.staples.com/product_454300), [Markertek](https://www.markertek.com/product/ergo-45-241-026/ergotron-lx-45-241-026-desk-mount-lcd-arm-for-screens-to-24-inch-silver), [Levata](https://www.levata.com/ergotron-45-241-026-stand)). The LX Pro is listed at 22 lb capacity and $199, but its minimum load is reported inconsistently (roughly 4 lb in some listings, 1.8 kg in one), and I did not reach Ergotron's own pages ([Staples](https://www.staples.com/ergotron-lx-pro-adjustable-monitor-arm-34-white-45-712-290/product_24701683), [Inet.se](https://www.inet.se/produkt/2302437/ergotron-lx-pro-desk-monitor-arm)). The 8.5 lb panel sits inside every reported range. Why not the roughly $40 Amazon Basics gas-spring arm (4.4 to 15.4 lb)? It has the capacity ([Slickdeals](https://slickdeals.net/f/18315928-amazon-basics-gas-spring-single-computer-monitor-arm-mount-up-to-27-monitors-16-79-free-shipping-w-prime-or-on-35)). The case for the Ergotron is rigidity under repeated poking, which the Humanscale M2.1 review flags as a weakness of lighter arms ([Work While Walking](https://www.workwhilewalking.com/humanscale-m2-monitor-arm-review)); no source tests touch-wobble on any arm, so that is my inference. This is the one place the budget rule is a judgment call: the $40 arm is the defensible frugal pick, and the $160 gap buys feel and durability, not capacity. Two mounting caveats: every clamp arm needs a rear lip or an edge under about 2.4 inches, and the only weighted-base articulating arm I found for a bare cabinet top is the Fellowes freestanding model, with conflicting load figures and no price ([Staples.ca](https://www.staples.ca/products/2944958-en-fellowes-professional-series-freestanding-single-monitor-arm)).

I recommend keeping the Pi, SSD and DAC on the shelf, not on the arm. Hanging them on the VESA plate adds weight and flexing cables to the moving part and lengthens the RCA run. Two thin cables (HDMI and USB) then travel along the arm. This is design judgment; the VESA brackets exist if you disagree ([Pi Hut](https://thepihut.com/collections/raspberry-pi-store/products/aluminium-vesa-raspberry-pi-5-case)).

The Pi's RAM is outside your budget but worth a word: one Electron client, librespot and the desktop fit in 4 GB, yet 8 GB is the comfortable pick and 16 GB is unjustified (a 4 GB board barely handled 40 browser tabs at 3.9 GB ([Tom's Hardware](https://tomshardware.com/raspberry-pi/raspberry-pi-5-4gb-versus-8gb))). NVMe boot is supported by default: flash the drive with Imager, and with no SD card inserted the Pi 5 boots from it (bootloader 2023-12-06 or later), while with an SD card present you change boot order in raspi-config ([Raspberry Pi docs](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/accessories/m2-hat-plus.html)). Leave the PCIe link at its default Gen 2 (about 450 MB/s); FLAC playback needs a tiny fraction of that, and Gen 3 is uncertified ([Raspberry Pi docs](https://www.raspberrypi.com/documentation/accessories/m2-hat-plus.html)). A fanless case was not found that also takes NVMe: the Flirc throttled after about 4 minutes of sustained load and reviewers report no GPIO or PCIe access ([bret.dk](https://bret.dk/flirc-raspberry-pi-5-case-review/)). The Argon's fan stays off at light load, so it should be silent for music, though it may spin during a library scan; that is an inference from stress-test data, not a measured idle.

## Audio: a $100 DAC is already past audibility

ASR's own D10s review calls -120 dB distortion "well below threshold of hearing" and the D10s measures 107 to 110 dB SINAD when the amp needs 1 V for full power ([ASR](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/topping-d10s-usb-dac-and-bridge-review.14859/)). The commonly quoted "transparent" lines of 100 dB SINAD (practical) and 116 dB (guaranteed) come from forum paraphrases of ASR's founder, not primary text, so read them as community position ([Audioholics forum](https://forums.audioholics.com/forums/goto/post?id=1461089)). The one controlled test I found, Meyer and Moran, found a 16-bit/44.1 kHz loop inaudible in high-resolution playback, though critics note its sources were mostly older SACD and DVD-A material ([AES](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=14195)). I picked the D10s because it is the only candidate whose ASR page I could read end to end. The SU-2 is a stronger pick on paper: an ASR database row lists 123 dB SINAD at $100, and its vendor specs give 2.3 Vrms on RCA ([ASR data list](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/asrdata/ElectronicsallList), [Bloom Audio](https://bloomaudio.com/products/smsl-su2)). I could not read its review text or confirm its power source, so buy whichever is in stock and verify. The Topping E30 II ($149, vendor-claimed 119 dB SINAD) buys 6 to 12 dB over the D10s that you cannot hear, and its stock status is unclear; spend nothing past about $100 here ([APOS](https://apos.audio/products/topping-e30-ii-dac-digital-to-analog-converter)).

The HAT route is real but loses on practicalities. HiFiBerry's DAC2 HD was measured by Archimago at about 2.04 Vrms with THD+N better than -90 dB and negligible jitter, "good enough" and at the level of $100-class USB DACs; it is I2S, so it sidesteps USB entirely ([Archimago](https://archimago.blogspot.com/2021/02/measurements-hifiberry-dac2-hd-hat-for.html)). Against it: Archimago tested Pi 3 and 4, not Pi 5; no source confirms an NVMe board and a DAC HAT stacking in a shared case; and several Pi 5 owners report over-current messages and glitching with some USB DACs at high rates ([Volumio forum](https://community.volumio.com/t/raspberrypi-thinks-my-benchmark-dac-is-an-adafruit-amp/70953?page=2)). That evidence mainly covers high-draw DACs such as the Benchmark. I found no Pi 5 dropout report for the D10s, SU-2 or E30 II, and the official 27 W supply lifts the USB budget to 1.6 A ([Raspberry Pi whitepaper](https://pip-assets.raspberrypi.com/categories/685-app-notes-guides-whitepapers/documents/RP-009856-WP-1-USB%20Power%20delivery%20on%20Raspberry%20Pi%205.pdf)). If the USB DAC glitches on arrival, the DAC2 HD or a powered hub is the fallback.

Use analog RCA and skip digital out. A 2 Vrms unbalanced output matches consumer line inputs, and the Digi2 Pro or a USB-to-S/PDIF box would only help if your amp's internal DAC measures better than a $100 stick, which is rare; I found no measurement of any amp's DAC to test that, so the exception is amp-specific features such as room correction. Run the DAC at fixed full output and set loudness with the amp's volume knob, which keeps every bit intact. Hum is the remaining risk: no Pi 5 evidence exists either way, and the passive isolator in the parts list is the cheap cure ([The Pi Hut](https://thepihut.com/products/argon-blstr-dac-with-ground-loop-isolator-for-argon-one-v3)).

## Software stack and the steps that matter

Install Raspberry Pi OS Trixie 64-bit with its default labwc Wayland desktop. The critical touch setting is in `~/.config/labwc/rc.xml`: the shipped touch entry uses `mouseEmulation="yes"`, which turns every touch into a mouse click and disables multitouch, and `no` enables real touch events ([CNX Software](https://cnx-software.com/2025/03/12/tips-to-use-a-touchscreen-display-with-raspberry-pi-os-in-2025/?amp=1), [Hudiy forum](https://hudiy.eu/community/viewtopic.php?p=878)). The exact attribute syntax for mapping touch to a specific output was not confirmed in my sources, so read `man labwc-config` on the Pi. With a single display attached, mapping should be unnecessary. For a software keyboard use squeekboard, since Onboard works poorly under labwc (CNX, above). Dragging and scrolling should work in an Electron client; this is a toolkit inference, and the documented counterexample is Supersonic, whose Fyne toolkit has an open touch-scroll bug ([Supersonic issue 626](https://github.com/supersonic-app/supersonic/issues/626)).

For the client, use **Feishin 1.17.0**, which ships official arm64 AppImage, .deb and tar.xz builds and an mpv backend ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/repos/jeffvli/feishin/releases/latest)). It has a fix for mpv failing to reinitialize after system wake in 1.17.0, relevant to a screen that sleeps, but 1.16.0 needed a next-day 1.17.0 for breaking bugs, so pin a release and update deliberately ([release notes](https://github.com/jeffvli/feishin/releases/tag/v1.17.0)). Install the .deb, point it at the system `mpv` (the README lists 0.35 and 0.36 as known good and 0.34 as broken; Trixie ships newer, so test), and add two server profiles: dubplate and the local server. Whether Feishin exposes mpv's exclusive-output option was not verified. Supersonic has the best mpv audio features but publishes no arm64 Linux build, and Aonsoku has arm64 builds but no confirmed mpv backend ([Supersonic releases](https://api.github.com/repos/supersonic-app/supersonic/releases), [Aonsoku](https://github.com/victoralvesf/aonsoku)). No source tests any of them on a Pi 5, so Feishin's fit is reasoned, not proven.

The local library needs a server because none of these clients plays a bare folder. Run **Navidrome** (v0.64.2, 2026-09-24) on the Pi over the synced folder; gonic (0.22.0) is the lighter alternate ([Navidrome releases](https://api.github.com/repos/navidrome/navidrome/releases), [gonic releases](https://api.github.com/repos/sentriz/gonic/releases)). The appliance distributions do not fit: moOde 10.3.6 is the strongest Pi 5 option but I found no OpenSubsonic support in it, and Volumio's Subsonic plugin targets an old API ([moOde release notes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/moode-player/moode/master/www/relnotes.txt), [Volusonic thread](https://community.volumio.com/t/plugin-volusonic/9347?page=7)).

For syncing, no mature OpenSubsonic bulk-sync tool exists, and the small scripts I found were not verified as maintained ([naps](https://github.com/PokerFacowaty/naps)). The `download` endpoint is one album per request, so a whole-library pull through it means iterating thousands of requests. The cleaner path is `rclone sync` over SFTP from the server's FLAC store, which is bit-exact and resumable, and rclone is already a pinned tool in the dubplate repo. That needs a file-level access path on the box, which is an owner decision on dubplate's side. Sync over Ethernet: Pi 5 Wi-Fi manages about 217 Mbps close to the router, so a 1 TB first sync takes 10-plus hours that way, against roughly a third of that on gigabit ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/news/raspberry-pi-5-wi-fi-faster)). Metal cases cut Wi-Fi throughput about 20% ([LinuxLinks](https://www.linuxlinks.com/sunfounder-pironman-5-review-raspberry-pi/5/)).

For Spotify, install **raspotify 0.48.3** (librespot 0.8.0, arm64 .deb, systemd unit) as the Connect receiver ([raspotify releases](https://api.github.com/repos/dtcooper/raspotify/releases)). librespot 0.8.0 fixed the Spotify-side breakage of November 2025 that silenced older versions, so keep it updatable ([Music Assistant issue](https://github.com/music-assistant/support/issues/4392)). **go-librespot 0.10.3** is the alternate: it has a documented PulseAudio backend (which PipeWire serves), a REST and WebSocket API, and very active releases, but only about 377 stars ([go-librespot](https://github.com/devgianlu/go-librespot)). Expect no lossless from either: Spotify began rolling out lossless to Premium in September 2025, but librespot's lossless request was closed "not planned", and licensed Connect devices get it while community receivers do not ([librespot #1580](https://github.com/librespot-org/librespot/issues/1580), [dCS forum](https://dcs.community/t/spotify-lossless-is-real-and-finally-rolling-out/8151)). Spotify tops out at 320 kbps Vorbis through these tools; your own FLAC library is where the fidelity is. The reasons for the community's conflicting explanations (DRM versus not exposed over Connect) are unsettled and none come from Spotify.

Bit-perfect output is a configuration of three layers. First, tell PipeWire which sample rates the DAC may run at, since `default.clock.allowed-rates` defaults to empty and the graph rate only switches while devices are idle ([pipewire.conf(5)](https://docs.pipewire.org/page_man_pipewire_conf_5.html)). A drop-in such as `~/.config/pipewire/pipewire.conf.d/10-rates.conf` with `context.properties = { default.clock.allowed-rates = [ 44100 48000 88200 96000 176400 192000 ] }` follows community practice ([EndeavourOS forum](https://forum.endeavouros.com/t/how-to-achieve-bit-perfect-audio-output/49760)); do not pin `default.clock.rate`. Second, keep player volume at 100% with no software gain or effects and use the amp for level. Third, confirm it worked while a 96 kHz track plays: `cat /proc/asound/card*/pcm*p/sub*/hw_params` should show the file's rate. That check is standard ALSA behaviour from general knowledge, not from a source I retrieved. The unavoidable limitation is sharing: when Feishin, raspotify and a browser all run through PipeWire, simultaneous streams are mixed and resampled, so bit-perfect holds only when one source plays at a time. For strict exclusivity, mpv's `--audio-device=alsa/hw:...` with exclusive mode bypasses PipeWire but blocks other sources; whether Feishin passes those mpv arguments is unverified. One-source-at-a-time is the practical answer for a stereo.

## Verify on arrival, in this order

The first four items are the ones the research could not settle; the rest are cheap sanity checks. Order matters because the earlier ones decide whether the later parts stay.

1. **Dell touch point count on Linux.** With the Pi on the monitor via the HDMI plus USB-A to USB-C pair, run `libinput debug-events` (or `evtest`) and put several fingers down. Count simultaneous slots; set `mouseEmulation="no"` first, then check pinch in Feishin. If only three register, decide whether that is enough for a music app (it should be) before keeping it. Test inside the return window.
2. **Touch after standby.** Let the screen blank, wake it, and confirm touch events still appear in `libinput debug-events`. No source documents this for an external USB touch monitor; the nearest evidence is laptop reports where events continue but input needs a re-login ([Framework forum](https://community.frame.work/t/touchscreen-stops-working-after-sleep/78580)). If touch dies, try unplugging and replugging the USB cable to see whether a reconnect recovers it, then consider a switched supply or turning off blanking.
3. **Arm availability and fit.** Confirm the LX Pro (or LX 45-241-026) is actually in stock, read Ergotron's own minimum load for it, and measure your shelf edge: a clamp needs a rear lip or an edge of 10 to 60 mm for the LX line. Confirm the Dell's VESA plate mounts without its stand and that the arm reaches arm's-length standing height.
4. **DAC power source and Pi 5 stability.** Check whether your chosen DAC (D10s, SU-2) draws power from USB or an external adapter, and confirm in `dmesg` that no over-current messages appear while a 192 kHz file plays for a few minutes on the 27 W supply. Neither the D10s nor SU-2 power source was confirmed in my sources.
5. **Output level and hum.** Play silence at the amp's working volume with the DAC at fixed output; if you hear hum, add the isolator.
6. **Bit-perfect check.** Play 44.1, 96 and 192 kHz files and read `hw_params` for each.
7. **Feishin on Pi OS.** Confirm touch scrolling, gapless playback and the mpv path work, that the system mpv version launches cleanly, and that two server profiles coexist.
8. **Spotify Connect.** Confirm raspotify appears in the Spotify app, plays through the same DAC, and does not fight Feishin for the device.
9. **Thermals and fan.** Watch temperature during a Navidrome library scan with the Argon case on.

## Conclusion

The research reshaped the question more than it answered it: the audio choice is easy and cheap, the display choice is a bet that a return window protects, and the software is a sound plan that nobody has verified on this hardware. Spending is justified on the Dell and, arguably, the Ergotron arm; every dollar past about $100 on the DAC, any digital-out gear, and any lossless-promising Spotify workaround buys nothing you can detect. The frugal build lands near $850 and the comfortable build near $1,000, so the budget does not need stretching past its floor, and the headroom beyond the floor is best left unspent.

The most useful unresolved fact is a single measurement you can take in five minutes: how many touch points the Dell reports under Linux and whether it survives standby. If either fails, the fallback path is the ViewSonic TD2455 or iiyama T2454MSC (both vendor-claimed for Linux), not a smaller screen. Everything else in the stack (Navidrome, rclone, raspotify, PipeWire rates) is replaceable in an afternoon, which is why the display, the arm and the DAC power source are the three decisions to verify before you commit.
